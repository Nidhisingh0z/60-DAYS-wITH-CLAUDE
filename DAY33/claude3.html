Learn to read media the way it was actually written — on purpose.

This isn't a quiz. There's no score for what you get "right." You'll look at headlines and posts that feel like the ones in your own feed, form your own honest reaction, and then we'll pull back the curtain on exactly how they were built to make you feel that way.

Concept 1 of 2

Headline Accuracy

A headline has one job: get you to click. The article underneath has a different job: tell you what actually happened. Those two jobs don't always agree — a headline can be technically true and still badly misleading, through exaggeration, missing context, or vague wording that lets your imagination fill in the scariest possible version of events.

This matters because most people never get past the headline. On social platforms, a large share of shared links are never even opened by the person sharing them — which means for most readers, the headline is the whole story, accurate or not.

In this challenge: read a real-feeling headline and article, judge it with your own gut, then compare your read to what's really going on underneath.

Concept 2 of 2

Emotional Manipulation

Content that makes you feel something strongly — fear, anger, hope, outrage — tends to get shared faster than content that just informs you. Algorithms notice this and show more of it to you, and creators know it too. That's not automatically dishonest, but it becomes a manipulation technique the moment the feeling is the point, and the facts are just the delivery vehicle.

This shows up everywhere: the forwarded family group-chat message, the "you won't believe this" reel, the post that tells you to share it before you can check it. Recognizing the technique doesn't mean the topic isn't real — it means you get to decide how to react, instead of being steered.

In this challenge: read a post, notice how it makes you feel and which words are doing that work, then see the technique behind the curtain.

    Three habits worth keeping

    • 1Pause before you share. Check the date, the source, and whether other outlets are reporting the same thing.
    • 2Read past the headline. Headlines are written to be clicked — the article is where accuracy actually has to hold up.
    • 3Notice your emotions. If something makes you feel a strong emotion fast — fear, anger, urgency — that's a signal to slow down, not speed up.
